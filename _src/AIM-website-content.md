Engineering

## Design for the failure path first, the happy path second.

This approach comes from systems where failure has real consequences: live voice calls that can't drop mid-conversation, healthcare workflows that have to keep working through network problems, and media pipelines where one failed stage shouldn't spoil the whole job.

Try the reliability lab See the stack

Principles

### Architecture principles

The same patterns show up in every project we build.

#### Local-first where it makes sense

Keep critical workflows close to where they run when that improves latency, privacy, resilience or cost. Use the cloud where it adds leverage, and keep control locally where it adds resilience.

#### Zero-trust boundaries

Components shouldn't trust each other just because they share an application or a network. Tenant scope is part of every key, query and cache entry.

#### Asynchronous execution

Long-running and failure-prone work moves into controlled worker pipelines instead of blocking the request path.

#### Redis as a systems primitive

Redis does much more than caching: queues, pub/sub, rate limiting, distributed coordination, circuit-breaker synchronisation, state, and memory/vector infrastructure.

#### Idempotency

At-least-once delivery is the realistic guarantee, so running the same job twice has to be safe. A retried webhook rehydrates the existing session, and re-uploading a call list never double-dials.

#### Backpressure

A system should know when it's overloaded. A reliable system doesn't accept unlimited work until it collapses; it queues within limits and then refuses work cleanly.

#### Graceful degradation

When a dependency fails, the whole system shouldn't fail with it. We rely on fallbacks, retries, bounded concurrency and controlled degradation.

#### Observability

Logs alone aren't enough. We combine metrics, structured logs, traces and operational state, because debugging a distributed system means reconstructing what actually happened.

Audit

### We document the limitations too

None of these systems is perfect. We'd rather write the limitations down than present an artificially polished picture.

#### APEX Connect

Strengths: at-least-once delivery, idempotency guards, Redis-synced circuit breaking, per-tenant capacity control, persistent queue/WAL state, plus chaos, stress and end-to-end tests.

Audit findings: persistence is complex, some behaviour fails open, the surface area is large, and files that look like credentials raise due-diligence concerns.

#### APEX HMS

Strengths: WAL and replay recovery, single-authority supervision, tamper-evident audit chains, durable outboxes.

Audit findings: still under active construction. 30 test suites: 28 passing, 2 failing, and 94 tests pending or skipped.

#### Shortz

Strengths: per-stage telemetry, failure classification, soak testing and failure-injection infrastructure.

Audit findings: best described as systems-oriented application engineering rather than a mature production service. Test coverage is uneven.

How I work

### I do my best work when I own the problem end to end

I like environments without a perfectly defined spec, where someone has to work out:

What should exist? How should it work? What can fail? How should it scale? How do we deploy it? How do we know when it breaks? How do we recover? What should we deliberately not build?

Whether a client hires one engineer or a full team, I stay accountable for the architecture. I prefer early-stage environments, where deep ownership and fast execution matter more than process.

Where this is going

### AI-native infrastructure, not apps with an AI button

I want to build systems where AI is part of the operating architecture and runs inside infrastructure that is controlled, observable and reliable. That means combining the flexibility of AI with the discipline of traditional systems engineering. Making a model respond is the easy part. The interesting challenge is making an AI-powered system:

reliable observable secure economical recoverable scalable

I'm an AI systems engineer and software architect who builds the infrastructure behind real-world software products, from the architecture down to the operational details: APIs, workers, queues, AI pipelines, databases, infrastructure, observability, deployment and failure handling.

FAQ

### Frequently asked questions

#### What is AIM?

AIM (AI Infrastructure & Machines), also known as AIM Studio, is an MSME registered by the Government of India (Udyam), based in Madhya Pradesh, with Ankit Panicker as CTO & AI Systems Engineer. It makes APEX Connect (SaaS) and AIM (a free AI operating layer), and it takes on B2B engineering engagements.

#### How is APEX Connect priced?

Per tenant, per month: ₹4,999 with your own SIP carrier, or an all-inclusive calling plan at ₹14,999 for 2,000 AI calls, ₹29,999 for 4,500 or ₹49,999 for 10,000. Business, Enterprise and White-label are custom. GST applies where relevant.

#### Which carriers and languages are supported?

Twilio, Telnyx, Vapi and Indian SIP trunk providers, plus the WhatsApp Business API. Calls are supported in English and Hindi.

#### What does "outside IR35" mean?

The engagement is a genuine business-to-business contract: fixed scope and milestone payments, the contractor carries the financial risk and provides their own tools, and there's no mutuality of obligation. You contract directly with AIM, an MSME registered by the Government of India (Udyam).

#### What is end-to-end product delivery?

AIM takes the product from architecture and system design through backend and frontend engineering, infrastructure (Terraform, Kubernetes), CI/CD and monitoring to production deployment, staffed with one engineer or a full team depending on the scope.

#### Can I hire one engineer or a whole team?

Yes. Hire a single dedicated engineer for a focused build, review or rescue, or a team sized to the scope for a full platform. The CTO stays accountable for the architecture either way.

#### Is AIM (Remote AI) really free?

Yes. It's free for Windows, Android and Chrome with no account. You bring a local model or your own cloud provider key.
